    ⓘ How the Bestiary Worksthe Foe Folio, at your fingertips
    

      
What this is: every creature in the Foe Folio — 390 of them — as searchable cards. The search box reads names and ability text, so "swallow whole" or "energy drain" finds every foe that does it; the filters narrow by type, size, environment, and CR band.

      
The cards: click a name to open its full statblock. Underlined terms are live — hover (or tap) any spell, feat, skill, or special ability for its rules text on the spot, no page-flipping. The CR chip shows the honest, ruled rating; where a rating was corrected, the calculus row carries the provenance.

      
The Rules Lens (the dropdown on the right) re-reads every statblock in your table's dialect — E&E native, Classic SRD, or Modern SRD. Conversions are approximate and E&E remains the truth; where the dialects genuinely diverge, the E&E variant appears beneath the official reading so you can see both.

      
⚙ Advance (inside each card) grows a creature by Hit Dice or a quick class silhouette, and the CR calculus re-prices it live — the number you see is the number you'd pay.

      
Fast fielding: set a CR band and an environment, and the list becomes tonight's menu.

    

  

  

    
    
    
    
    
    
    
  

  





  

    ⓘ How the Forge Worksencounters priced honestly — GMG pp.39-41
    

      
What this is: the encounter builder. Three panels: who's playing, who's opposing, and the honest reading of what happens when they meet.

      
The Party: import your players straight from the Character Generator — a Save All file or a single hero's export both work — or add names and levels by hand. Tick Favored/Heroic attributes if your table rolled hot (+1 party CR, GMG p.39), and use Fine-tune ±1 as your table's real results dictate (p.40). The Familiar computes the party CR from there.

      
The Opposition: search the Bestiary and field foes into the tray — singles or squads. Counts multiply into the math automatically.

      
The Reading: the meter compares the Encounter Level against your party and says what the math says — a fair fight, a breather, or a funeral. That honesty is the point: adjust until the reading matches the night you intend. Then roll treasure (the printed rating sets the default; double rolls twice), name the encounter, and save it — saved encounters are what Table Ops fields onto the combat rail.

      
Worth knowing: a dragon played with its wings and wits fights a band above its printed number — the Bestiary's dragon doctrine cards have the details.

    ⓘ How Table Ops Worksthe session, while it happens
    

      
What this is: the live-play desk — a session log on the left, a combat rail on the right. Everything you record here becomes the session's transcript.

      
The Session: name the campaign and the session, then log as you go with one tap — 🎬 Scene when the party moves somewhere new, 💬 Quote when a player says the thing, 📝 Note for rulings and reminders, 💰 Loot when treasure changes hands. Type in the quick box, press the button, keep playing. The registers field takes tone tags ("gothic", "industrial-horror") — they'll style the Chronicler's retelling of this transcript when that service arrives.

      
The Combat Rail: field a saved Forge encounter (hit points arrive from the statblocks), pull in the party with one button, and add ad-hoc combatants as the night invents them. Sort by initiative, tick damage on the rail, and advance rounds — round changes are logged automatically.

      
Export downloads the whole session as a file — your record, on your machine, nothing kept anywhere else. New session archives the current log and opens a fresh page.

    ⓘ How the Stage Worksthe world as an opponent — from the GMG
    

      
What this is: the environment reference — travel and movement, terrain, weather, extreme effects, and dungeon features, harvested from the GM's Guide into cards. When the world itself is the obstacle, the ruling lives here.

      
Using it: search anything ("avalanche", "forced march", "quicksand") or filter by the tag dropdown. Terrain cards carry their full feature tables — chances, DCs, and footnotes as printed — so a wilderness crossing runs off one open card.

      
Pairs well with: the Loom's premises (the Crossing and the Deluge skeletons in particular are played almost entirely from this tab) and the Forge — terrain is the cheapest difficulty dial a GM owns.

    

  

  

    
    
    
  

  





  

    ⓘ How the Library Worksthe glossary behind every statblock
    

      
What this is: the rules glossary — special abilities and qualities (darkvision, damage reduction, energy drain, regeneration, fear and its kin) as searchable reference cards.

      
The quiet trick: these same entries power the hover layer everywhere else in the Familiar — when an underlined term in a Bestiary statblock pops up its rules text, it's the Library answering. Come here when you want the entry at full length, or to browse what a term's neighbors do.

    

  

  

    
    
  

  





  

    ⓘ How the Academy Workscasting your NPCs
    

      
What this is: the class reference, built for a GM's two questions. By class answers "what does this class actually give?" — for reading your players' sheets and statting named NPCs. By role is the casting-director view: start from the job the story needs — the healer, the face, the infiltrator — and see which classes play it, so the quartermaster you invented mid-sentence gets a competent chassis in seconds.

      
Pairs well with: the Loom — when a woven premise hands you a patron, a rival, or a serpent in the court, this tab casts the part.

    

  

  

    
    
    
  

  





  
  

    ⓘ How the Loom Worksfive minutes, no prior GMing required
    

      
What this is: a plot generator for Game Masters. You tell the Loom roughly what kind of story you want; it hands you back a complete scenario premise — who the party faces, what's at stake, and the complication that makes it a story rather than an errand. Use it to break a blank page, to prep a one-shot in ten minutes, or to surprise yourself out of a rut.

      
The skeleton (the first dropdown) is the story's engine — the one sentence that survives when every detail is stripped away. "The party must obtain something someone doesn't want them to have" is a skeleton; a dragon's hoard, a kidnapped heir, and a rival company's prototype are all skins stretched over it. The Loom carries seventeen, from the classic five scenario shapes in the GM's Guide (pp.15-16) to courtroom dramas, cons, and revolutions. Each one notes which GMG archetype it belongs to, and offers a few sentences of table advice once it's on the frame.

      
The threads (the labeled rows beneath) are that skeleton's moving parts — the prize, the opposition, the method, and so on. Each thread is a dropdown you can set by hand, a 🎲 that rolls just that one thread, and a lock. Changing a single thread re-skins the whole plot: the same Taking is a heist with one method and a siege with another.

      
The Weave! button rolls every unlocked thread at once and draws a twist. This is the Loom's rhythm: weave, read the premise, 🔒 lock the threads you like, weave again for the rest. Three or four passes usually lands on something you want to run. (🔓 means the thread rerolls; 🔒 means it holds.)

      
The twist is an optional complication drawn from a deck of sixteen — the employer's hidden agenda, the rival crew, the deadline that was never real. Each twist wears its family on a chip — Misdirection, Complication, Nemesis, or Crisis of Conscience — so you know at a glance whether it bends the truth, the odds, the enemies, or the heroes. One twist is plenty; a premise with three twists is a GM with a headache.

      
Subplots are personal threads, dealt one per hero — a temptation, a debt, an old vow. They run alongside the main plot across sessions, and they're where the inward story (what the GM's Guide calls being-vs-themself) lives in group play. Deal them at a campaign's start and let them simmer.

      
The genre skin dresses the premise for your world and quotes the Six-Scales settings that world implies (after the GMG's p.56 table) — pick "Classic Medieval Fantasy" and the same skeleton wears crowns and crypts; pick "Mecha & Starship Warfare" and it wears steel. The register declares drama or comedy up front, which the GM's Guide (p.15) recommends telling your players before the dice come out. The Flip turns any plot inside out: the party isn't running the heist, they're guarding against it.

      
Fate's d20 hands the whole choice to the dice. A roll of 1–17 picks a skeleton and weaves it on the spot. On 18–19 the Loom deals a Red Herring: the premise you see is a façade, and the quest's true skeleton waits in the Beneath the weave box — GM's eyes only. Run the façade honestly through Act I and let the truth surface at the first big reveal. On a natural 20 the tale lands Close to Home: the Loom names one of your imported heroes (or tells you to roll among the players) and the premise grows out of that hero's own history. Rarest of all, a 20 can deal a herring of its own — a quest wearing a face from a hero's past, and even the face is a mask.

      
From premise to session: open the Three-Act Scaffold under the premise and sketch one line per act — the hook, the two reveals, the finale. Then ⧉ Copy carries the whole weave into your notes. That's it: a skeleton, a few threads, a twist, three acts — you have run a session before you knew these words for it.

      
Try it now: press 🧵 Weave! once. Read what came out loud, in your best narrator voice. If any part of it makes you grin — lock it, and weave the rest until the grin spreads.

    

  

  

    
    
    
    
    
    
    
    
  

  

  





  

    ⓘ How the Adjudicator Worksrulings, with receipts
    

      
What this is: the deck of table-ready rulings — the calls that come up mid-session (massive damage, grapple, the horror-save flow, the stellar combat round, dragon doctrine) pre-adjudicated, each card citing its page so you can show the receipt when a player asks.

      
Using it: search by the word your table just said. The cards open pre-expanded because when you need one, you need it now. The deck grows as the rulings ledger does — if a call you make keeps recurring, it's a candidate for a card.
